What makes an A2L burn

By Shameen · HVAC service tech · Nov 9, 2026

02/05 lesson08min
Warm-up: 3 quick guesses

Guess before you read. Getting one wrong is fine. It makes the answer stick when you get to it below. Nothing here is scored.

A lot of techs think the safety class is the engineers' business. We just match the refrigerant on the data plate and go. I used to look at it that way too. But when I sat down with the standard, my biggest takeaway was simple: the class is about safety. It tells you how toxic the gas is, how easily it burns, what PPE to wear, and what to watch out for so the guy next to you goes home too.

This lesson covers the safety classes, how refrigerants get their names, why the oil matters as much as the gas, and how the lab decides what burns, how much refrigerant a room can take, and what it takes to light it.

1The letter and the number

Every refrigerant gets a safety class from ASHRAE Standard 34. Its international twin is ISO 817. The edition I studied is ANSI/ASHRAE 34-2019 / ISO 817-2020. CHECK newer edition

  • A or B: the cutoff is the occupational exposure limit (OEL), the amount a worker can breathe day after day. 400 ppm or more is A. Under 400 ppm is B.
  • 1: no flame spreads in the test.
  • 2L: it can burn, but the flame crawls: 10 cm/s or less (about 4 inches a second). Hard to light, weak flame.
  • 2: burns, faster flame.
  • 3: lights easily and burns hot, like propane. It can light at a low amount in the air, or it puts out a lot of heat when it burns.
Build a refrigerant class

Pick a letter and a number. See what that class means on the job.

Letter = toxicity
Number = flammability
Don't mix up the two numbers. The number in the safety class (the 3 in A3) is flammability. The R-number on the tank (R-290, R-600a, R-410A) is not. It comes from the chemistry. R-410A has a big number and doesn't burn. R-32 has a small one and does.

What the class changes for me

ClassOn the job
A1Still pools low and pushes out oxygen. Still makes toxic gas in a flame. Gloves and goggles.
A2LAll of that, plus: kill ignition sources, keep air moving, A2L-rated tools, detector running.
A3Tiny charges only. Treat every leak like a gas leak.
BBreathing it is the danger. Know the exits, ventilation and respirator plan before you open anything.

2Read the R-number

The name on the tank is called the designation. ASHRAE 34 assigns it, and it's built from the refrigerant's chemical formula. So the name actually tells you what's in the molecule, if you know the trick.

The rule of 90

For simple refrigerants (two or three digits), add 90. The three digits you get are the number of carbon, hydrogen and fluorine atoms.

R-32 + 90122 Carbon1 Hydrogen2 Fluorine2

R-32 is CH₂F₂. Try R-134a: 134 + 90 = 224, so 2 carbon, 2 hydrogen, 4 fluorine. Try R-22: 22 + 90 = 112, so 1 carbon, 1 hydrogen, 2 fluorine, and the leftover spot is chlorine. That chlorine is why R-22 hurt the ozone layer.

The series

NumberWhat it is
R-4xxZeotropic blend (has glide). R-410A, R-454B, R-452B.
R-5xxAzeotropic blend (acts like one refrigerant). R-502, R-507A.
R-6xxOrganic, like hydrocarbons. R-600a isobutane.
R-7xxInorganic. 700 + the molecule's weight: ammonia R-717, CO₂ R-744.
R-1xxxFour digits = a double bond. The HFOs, like R-1234yf.

The letters

  • Capital letter after a blend (R-454A, B, C): same ingredients, different recipe. R-454B is R-32 and R-1234yf at about 69/31. The B has nothing to do with toxicity.
  • Small letter (R-134a, R-1234yf): same atoms, arranged a different way.
  • Prefix (HFC-134a, HCFC-22): same refrigerant as R-134a or R-22. The prefix just tells you the chemical family. You'll see it in rules and on spec sheets.

3Keep the oil dry

The same page that teaches the names also hammers on oil, and I'm glad it does. R-454B and R-32 systems run POE oil, just like R-410A. POE pulls water out of the air. Here's what I wrote down:

  1. 15 minutes. Don't leave POE open to the air for more than about 15 minutes. That goes for the compressor, the piping, and the oil jug. Past that you're working backwards. CHECK your compressor maker's number
  2. A deep vacuum won't fix wet POE. You hear this in the field all the time: "just pull a good vacuum and it'll dry out." A vacuum pulls free water out of the system. It can't undo water that has already reacted with POE. That reaction makes acid, and the acid stays.
  3. Moisture + air + other contaminants = trouble. Mix them with heat and you get a chemical reaction: acid and sludge that eat windings and plug metering devices.
  4. If you suspect acid, test the oil. An acid test kit costs less than a compressor. Don't guess by smell.
  5. Contaminated oil has to come out. Flush where it's allowed, change the oil, new filter driers, then retest. Follow the equipment maker. Some systems, like many VRF, don't allow add-on driers or flush chemicals in the field piping.

Real talk: you'll get pressure to hurry. Sometimes it's sales, sometimes it's your supervisor. Take your time anyway, and be ready to explain why. Cap the lines, keep the oil sealed, do the acid test. Ten extra minutes now beats a callback and a second compressor. We all want the company to make money. But I'd rather be known as the sharp tech whose jobs don't come back.

Something nobody in charge ever told me: think about your future self. When I'm working on a unit, I ask how I want it running five, seven, ten years from now. Maybe you're the one who gets the call when it doesn't.

4They test it. They don't guess.

The classes aren't made up out of thin air. Each refrigerant gets tested in a lab. The flammability test ASHRAE uses is a modified version of ASTM E681: a glass flask, a set mix of refrigerant and air, a spark, and a camera watching whether the flame spreads. CHECK test details

Three lab numbers decide where a refrigerant lands:

  • LFL (lower flammability limit): the least refrigerant in the air that can carry a flame through an even mix. It's given as a percent of the air by volume. Below it, the flame can't propagate, meaning spread out from where it started. Memory hook: Lowest Fire Level, like the least wood that still keeps a campfire going. R-32 is about 14% by volume, R-454B about 12%.
  • Heat of combustion: how much heat comes off when it burns.
  • Burning velocity: how fast the flame moves. This is what split old class 2 in two. A class 2 refrigerant with a flame of 10 cm/s or less is 2L, "mildly flammable." Mild here means gentle by nature: it's hard to light and the flame is slow, not that it's safe.

"Even A1 can burn"

A1 means no flame spread under the test conditions: about 140°F at normal air pressure. It doesn't mean it can never burn. Push it past that, like high pressure mixed with air, and some A1 refrigerants can become combustible. That's one more reason you never pressure test with refrigerant and air. Use dry nitrogen. And in a torch flame, any of them breaks down into toxic gas. CHECK R-410A specifics

The lab isn't your mechanical room

Same as equipment ratings: the lab is a perfect, controlled setup, and the field never is. The LFL test uses an even mix of refrigerant and air. A real leak isn't even. It sinks and pools at the floor. So a room can be far below the LFL on average while the bottom few inches, right where the pilot light sits, are above it.

Do I really need to know this?

Honestly, on most calls you're thinking about airflow and the compressor, not refrigerant science. I get it. Call this need-to-know. But you use these lab numbers every day without seeing them:

Lab numberWhere it shows up on my job
LFLThe leak sensor (RDS) trips at about 25% of LFL. The nameplate's minimum room area comes from the LFL and the charge. CHECK

5How much is too much in a room: RCL

RCL (refrigerant concentration limit) is the most refrigerant allowed in an occupied space. Memory hook: Room's Cap Limit, like the max occupancy sign on the wall.

ASHRAE works out three limits and the RCL is the lowest of them. The tightest one wins:

ToxicityATEL OxygenODL FireFCL
  • ATEL (acute toxicity exposure limit): how much you can breathe in a short burst before it hurts you. Hook: too much, too fast.
  • ODL (oxygen deprivation limit): how much refrigerant before it pushes out enough oxygen to hurt you (asphyxiation).
  • FCL (flammable concentration limit): a safety margin under the LFL, so a leak in the room stays well short of burning. CHECK exact %

You'll also see IDLH (immediately dangerous to life or health), a level where you could be killed or badly hurt, or not be able to get yourself out. Hook: I Don't Linger Here. If you're anywhere near it, you leave.

A real one from my own work. An R-410A VRF system, well over 100 lb of charge. The indoor unit sat above the ceiling tiles in a small bathroom. The evap fittings were opened, and refrigerant rushed out for about 20 minutes. On VRF, a leak at one indoor unit can pull from the whole system, so that wasn't just one coil's worth.

Run the numbers on that bathroom

  • Room about 5 × 8 × 8 ft = about 320 ft³.
  • R-410A's RCL is about 26 lb per 1,000 ft³, which works out to about 14% of the air. For this room that's only about 8 lb. CHECK
  • 8 lb of R-410A vapor takes up about 45 ft³, roughly a seventh of that room. At 14% refrigerant, oxygen drops to about 18%, already under OSHA's 19.5% line.
  • In a 20-minute rush from a 100+ lb system, you're likely far past that, and worst of all up in the ceiling cavity where your head is.

What I'd do now: isolate the unit with the service valves first, before opening anything. If it gets away from you, stop, step out, and ventilate. Don't stay in the room trying to save the charge. This is also why ASHRAE 15 checks a VRF system's charge against the smallest room it serves.

Where this hits my job: RCL and the charge size decide how much refrigerant a system can have for the room it serves. That's why the nameplate lists a minimum room area, and why a big leak in a small room is a bigger deal than the same leak on a roof.

6Spark, heat and smoke

So far: what class it is, how much it takes to burn, and how much a room can hold. This last part is what it takes to light it, how hot it burns, and what the smoke does to you.

MIE: how big a spark

MIE (minimum ignition energy) is the smallest electrical spark that can light a refrigerant and air mix. It's measured in millijoules (mJ). The bigger the MIE, the harder it is to light.

Propane (A3)~0.25mJ R-32 (A2L)~30+mJ R-1234yf (A2L)1,000smJ

Rough numbers to show the gap, not exact values. CHECK

Most 2Ls are hard to light because their MIE is high, and they also need a lot of refrigerant in the air (above the LFL) before they can burn at all. Everyday stuff like a cordless drill, a toaster, a hair dryer or an electric heater generally won't light a 2L. CHECK But "generally" isn't "never." An open flame, a pilot light or a glowing hot surface is a different story. Treat those as real ignition sources every time.

HOC: how hot it burns

Heat of combustion (HOC) is how much heat comes off when it burns. 2Ls give off a lot less heat than A3s like propane, so if one does light, the fire is less likely to keep itself going and spread.

The smoke: HF and COF2

This part applies to every fluorocarbon refrigerant: CFCs, HCFCs, HFCs, and yes, A1s like R-22, R-134a and R-410A. When any of them burns or breaks down on a hot surface, it can make:

  • HF (hydrogen fluoride): turns into hydrofluoric acid on anything wet, like your eyes, throat and lungs. In air it can show up as white fumes.
  • COF2 (carbonyl fluoride): a short-lived toxic gas. It reacts with moisture and turns into more HF.

The good news: these byproducts usually smell sharp and sting your nose and throat, so you get a warning. If you smell that, get out, ventilate, and get medical attention if anyone breathed it in. Don't push through it to finish the job.

Field example: this is one reason you don't sweat out an old filter drier with a torch. Heating it puts leftover refrigerant and oil in the flame. It also drives the trapped water and acid back into the system. Recover the charge, cut the drier out with a tubing cutter, and braze the new one in with nitrogen flowing.

Brazing: torch and flux

A question I asked myself while studying: does the flux I braze with change any of this? Not the refrigerant's MIE, LFL or heat of combustion. Those belong to the refrigerant. But brazing still ties in three ways:

  1. The torch blows past every limit. R-32 can light by itself, with no spark, at about 1,200°F (its auto-ignition temperature). Brazing heat runs about 1,050 to 1,600°F. So hot copper alone can light it. That's why on A2L you recover, purge with nitrogen, and braze with nitrogen flowing. No refrigerant in the line means nothing to light. CHECK
  2. Flux fumes are their own hazard. White brazing flux has fluoride compounds. When it cooks off, the fumes irritate your nose, throat and lungs. Keep air moving, keep your face out of the smoke, and read the flux's SDS.
  3. Flux inside the system turns into acid. Boric acid and fluoride plus water (you thin it with water) is the same moisture-and-acid mess from section 3. Put a thin coat only on the outside of the male fitting, never inside. Copper to copper with phos-copper rod doesn't need flux. Save flux for brass, steel and mixed metals.
